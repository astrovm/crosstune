Crosstune Privacy Policy

Last updated: October 30, 2026

In short

  • No account, ads or analytics.
  • Your history stays on your phone. Crosstune's only server gets the problem reports you send.
  • Links you convert go only to the music services involved.
  • Recognizing a song sends Shazam only a fingerprint of the sound, never the audio.
  • Clear history anytime in the app.
  • AI features are optional. They send lyrics to OpenAI only after you sign in with ChatGPT.

Details

Crosstune is an Android app developed by astrovm that opens music links in the music app or website you choose. This policy explains what information the app uses and where it goes.

No account, advertising, or analytics

Crosstune does not require an account and does not include advertising, analytics, or a crash-reporting service. The developer does not operate a server that receives your music links or listening history. Crosstune does not sell your information. The developer's only server receives the problem reports you choose to send, described below.

Information on your device

The app stores your settings and recent resolved links on your device. Recent entries can include the source link, title, artist, artwork URL, and prepared destination links. These preferences and history are excluded from the app's Android cloud-backup and device-transfer rules.

Crosstune reads a link you paste, share to it, or open through Android link handling. Its clipboard shortcuts read clipboard text when you invoke them. It checks which supported music apps are installed so it can offer them as destinations. That information stays on your device.

Requests to music services

To resolve a music link, Crosstune requests its public page or public API from the source service. It also loads cover artwork from the service's image servers. Supported source services include Spotify, YouTube, YouTube Music, Apple Music, Deezer, TIDAL, SoundCloud, Bandcamp, and Audiomack. Shazam links are read through Apple Music's lookup, and older ones through Shazam's public API first. Shared Google results are followed on Google to read the song's title.

To open the item itself, the app sends the item's title and artist to the destination's search service: Apple Music, Deezer, YouTube Music, YouTube, Bandcamp, TIDAL, SoundCloud, Audiomack, or Qobuz. A song on Spotify is found through ListenBrainz instead: its title and artist go to ListenBrainz, which knows which Spotify songs go by that name, and Spotify's public pages for those songs are read. A song ListenBrainz doesn't know yet is looked for among its artist's most played songs: the artist's name goes to MusicBrainz, and Spotify's public page for the artist is read. With links going straight to an app and “Play right away” off, a search opens there instead, and nothing is sent. When you open a destination, the selected app or website receives the destination link or search terms. When you use Play all or Shuffle on a playlist or album, the app sends each song’s title and artist to YouTube Music’s search, then sends the list of videos found to YouTube, which returns a temporary playlist. In Spotify, each song is found through ListenBrainz instead, as its turn comes. Custom destinations receive the search terms in the URL template you configure. Sharing a result sends it to the app or recipient you select.

When you type a song's name instead of a link, Crosstune sends the name to Deezer's and Apple's public song searches, and shows the songs they find, so you can open the song you meant without its link. When you ask for a song's lyrics or chords, the app sends the song's title and artist to services that need no account or key: LRCLIB, YouTube Music, NetEase Cloud Music and Letras for lyrics, Ultimate Guitar, Cifra Club, LaCuerda and U-FRET for chords, and Apple's song search and song pages to find the song's original name, or who first sang and wrote a cover's song. When you turn on Translation in the lyrics, the app sends their lines to Google Translate, or to MyMemory when Google Translate can't translate them, translation services that need no account, unless ChatGPT translates them, as described below. Android tells which language a song is in on your phone, and lyrics already in the language you translate into, or in one you chose not to translate, aren't sent. Readings, like kana over kanji, are worked out on your phone and sent nowhere. A word you tap for its meaning is sent the same way. If the app is in English, an English word is looked up in Wiktionary's dictionary instead. Lines you save stay on your phone. For Japanese, a bigger dictionary, UniDic, is downloaded once from Maven Central, only on Wi-Fi or another network that isn't metered, which, like any request, exposes your IP address to Maven Central.

If you let lyrics follow the song playing in your music app, Android asks you to allow Crosstune's notification access. Crosstune uses it only to see which song your music apps say they're playing and how far into it they are, so the lyrics can follow along and a line you tap can move the song there. It never reads your notifications, and none of this leaves your phone. You can turn it off in Android's settings at any time. When you practice saved lines, Crosstune also asks your music app to play the song each line is from, at that line. Crosstune's player uses the same access to show the song and what plays next, and to pause, skip, shuffle, repeat or move it, press the app's own like, or pause it on a sleep timer, in your music app.

Like other internet requests, these requests expose your IP address and normal request information to the contacted service. Those services may retain request logs and process information under their own privacy policies. The developer doesn't control how they process or keep that data. A music service may require its own account or subscription after you open it.

Song recognition

When you ask Crosstune to recognize a song, it records sound from the microphone only while it listens, for up to 12 seconds, and stops as soon as it finds the song. On your phone, it turns the sound into a fingerprint of its loudest points. Only that fingerprint goes to Shazam, never the audio, and the recording isn't saved. Crosstune doesn't send your location. Shazam answers with the song's title, artist, cover and Apple Music ID, and Crosstune then looks the song up like a pasted link. It also asks Apple, by that ID, how long the recording is, so the lyrics it finds are timed for that recording, and does the same for each song it names while listening along. Like other requests, this one exposes your IP address to Shazam, which processes it under its own privacy policy. If Crosstune can't reach Shazam, it opens the Shazam app or the Google app instead, when you have one; they listen under their own privacy policies.

Lyrics can also listen along: right after Crosstune recognizes a song, or when you tap the microphone in the lyrics. While the lyrics are on screen, Crosstune keeps listening the same way, sending Shazam a new fingerprint every few seconds, never audio, to stay in time with the song and to catch the next one. Listening stops as soon as you close the lyrics, turn it off, or leave the app. While the lyrics float over your other apps, it goes on listening until you close them. When microphone access is already allowed, lyrics can briefly listen to correct and remember the intro timing for that recording. They hear the sound your phone plays, through headphones too, and use the microphone only if your phone won't share it. On phones with 3 GB of memory or more, lyrics also hear what your phone plays, 20 seconds at a time, to find when each line is sung and move it there: through the song the first time it plays from the start, and once whenever you pick it up partway or skip within it. That happens on your phone, and the sound isn't saved or sent. The speech model it uses, whisper.cpp's, is downloaded once from Hugging Face, only on Wi-Fi or another network that isn't metered, which, like any request, exposes your IP address to Hugging Face.

ChatGPT

Crosstune's AI features are optional and only work after you sign in with your own paid ChatGPT plan. You sign in on OpenAI's site, in your browser. Crosstune keeps the sign-in on your phone, leaves it out of backups, and sends it only to OpenAI. Signing out deletes it.

While you're signed in, Crosstune sends OpenAI what each feature needs: a song's title, artist and lyrics to translate or explain them, correct Japanese readings, write lyrics found in Latin letters in Japanese or Korean, or pick words worth learning; a word and its line to look it up; the questions you ask about a line or a song; and a description you type, or a song, to find songs that match. OpenAI handles them under its own privacy policy, and they count toward your plan's usage.

When you open a song's meaning, Crosstune also sends its title and artist to MusicBrainz, a music database run by MetaBrainz that needs no account or key, to get the year it came out and its album, so the meaning gives the same ones every time. What it answers is kept on your phone, so a song is asked about once.

Casting to a TV

In the Google Play and GitHub builds you can cast a song to Crosstune on your Android TV or Google TV. Google Cast, part of Google Play Services, looks for TVs on your local network and carries the cast. When you cast, Crosstune sends the TV the song's title, artist, link and cover, and then what you ask of it: pause, play, skip, move, translation and readings. The TV tells your phone what it plays. This goes between your phone and your TV, never to the developer, and Google handles it under its own privacy policy. The build without Google Play Services has no casting and sends nothing of the kind.

Permissions and security

Crosstune uses Android's internet permission to retrieve music information and artwork, and reads the network state to tell when the phone is offline. It asks for the microphone permission the first time you recognize a song or open lyrics, and uses the microphone only while listening. If you float lyrics over other apps, it asks to display over other apps and, once, to show a notification, which keeps them up and lets you unlock or close them. It does not request location, camera, contacts, or advertising-ID permissions. Built-in music-service requests use HTTPS. If you configure a custom destination, choose a trusted HTTPS address. MilkDrop visuals also use the microphone permission to read the sound your phone plays, through Android; it isn't recorded or sent anywhere. If you use Shizuku or root, tapping Set everything up or Fix all asks for its permission, which Crosstune uses only to change Android's link settings, microphone, lyrics access and display over other apps for you.

Retention and deletion

You can clear recent history in the app. Clearing Crosstune's app storage in Android Settings removes its local settings and history. The developer holds no server-side account or listening-history record to delete. Requests already received by third-party music services are subject to those services' retention and deletion policies. Reports carry nothing that tells who sent them, so one can't be found to delete it on request. Each is deleted after 180 days.

Children

Crosstune does not ask users for their age or personal profile information and does not knowingly collect children's personal information on a developer-operated server. Linked music services have their own age requirements and privacy policies. Parents and guardians should review those before a child uses them.

Contact and changes

For privacy questions, contact crosstune@4st.li. If you email the developer, the email service processes your address and message so the developer can respond. Please don't include sensitive information you don't need to share.

Report a problem, in the lyrics, sends the developer's server, when you tap Send, what you write, the Crosstune and Android versions and, for the song, its title, artist and length, where its lyrics came from and how they're timed, the lines you put in time by hand, and the app Crosstune follows. The server runs on Cloudflare, which handles the connection, your IP address included, under its own privacy policy. The server doesn't keep your IP address. Only the developer reads reports, and they're never made public. If you turn on Report song problems automatically in Settings, or say yes when Crosstune offers it, Crosstune sends the same details, without a comment, when it finds a song with no lyrics, no timing or no chords, when you put a song's words in time by hand, or when the singing it hears is off from the words. Each song's problem goes once, and you can turn it off in Settings at any time. In Settings, Report a problem opens a GitHub issue or an email instead, filled in with the Crosstune and Android versions, which you can change and send yourself, and GitHub or your email service handles it under its own privacy policy.
